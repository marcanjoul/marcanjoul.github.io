AirBorder

Flight apps tell you when your plane leaves. AirBorder tells you how much free time you have before your next gate closes, and what fits in it.

01 · The problem

A departure time is not a deadline you can plan around

On a layover, “my flight leaves at 18:00” doesn't tell you when to stop eating. The real deadline is when the gate closes, and between you and it sit a walk, maybe a security or border check, maybe baggage, and flight information that can change while you're sitting down.

Travelers combine all of that in their heads to decide one thing: what can I do next, and when do I have to leave? When the plan breaks, the cost is real. In 2024, 21.9% of scheduled U.S. domestic flights didn't arrive on time, counting cancellations and diversions.¹ The two travelers we interviewed described lost rest, missed commitments and extra costs.

What exists

Flighty's Connection Assistant

Estimates the steps of a connection (deplaning, immigration, baggage, security, getting to the gate) and updates the status as conditions change. It overlaps the most with AirBorder, so it's our main comparison.

The gap we're testing

What about the time in between?

Knowing a route takes 12 minutes doesn't tell you whether to cut lunch short, stop working or skip the city visit. AirBorder ties the activity you chose to the steps you still have to do, and says which constraint changed when the plan stops working.

Our research question

How can an interface make the next feasible action understandable when time, access and information change together?

We aren't claiming AirBorder beats Flighty. Comparing the two on matched tasks is part of the evaluation we've planned. TripIt Pro and the Fly Delta app were reference points too.

02 · Research

Two interviews, read for what people did, not only what they said

We interviewed travelers through a digital questionnaire our team built. It branched into follow-up questions based on earlier answers and included interactive interface tasks, so we could see what people clicked as well as what they told us. Both participants completed it on their own.

Participant R1

18–24, computer science student, 3–5 flights in the past two years. Prefers map-first guidance.

Participant R2

18–24, computer science student, 3–5 flights in the past two years. Prefers camera guidance with the map hidden until opened.

Two people with nearly the same profile (students, CS, English-speaking, iPhone users) is a small, narrow sample. These findings are hypotheses to test, not conclusions about travelers in general.

What we found

Finding 1 · the one that changed the design most

“Easy” didn't mean they found it

Both travelers rated expanding the map as very easy. Yet neither used the dedicated map-expansion control first; both reached for the camera. After a gate change, R2 couldn't find the action that leads to the new gate and wrote:

“miss the map directing to new gate”

Design response: route actions are explicit and always visible, and we measure whether people find a control separately from how easy they say it was.

Both wanted to know what changed

Both put leave-by time, routes and explanations of changes first, and both picked “What changed and when” as what they wanted after an update.

Response: a change shows the old value struck through next to the new one, on every tab.

Same need, different guidance

R1 wanted the map first; R2 wanted the camera. Both wanted the app to remember their choice.

Response: both modes are always available, and the app remembers which one you used.

Disruption costs more than time

R1 described stress and lost rest. R2 described extra costs and missed commitments too.

Response: recovery guidance covers practical support, not just new times.

Their tools showed a gap too. Both used Google Flights, airline or airport apps and ChatGPT. R2 rated ChatGPT very helpful but also said its advice was sometimes outdated and took effort to move into an itinerary. And both preferred free or included facility access, like rest seating or a lounge through their ticket.

03 · Design decisions

Four decisions, each tied to something we heard

Decision 1

Count down to the gate closing, not the departure

Problem: both travelers put leave-by time first, and a departure time isn't the moment you have to stop.

Decision: the Flight tab leads with one sentence: 5h 25m before Gate 105 closes. The number is time left until the gate closes, the deadline that actually matters.

Decision 2

A plan comes with a time to leave

Problem: R1 lost the activity time they wanted; R2 works and rests during layovers. Picking a place is easy. Knowing when to walk away from it isn't.

Decision: place cards on the Layover tab show walk time and opening details before you add them, and the lounge card says when you'd have to leave (17:01). Once added, the plan becomes a timeline on the Flight tab (Now → Leave → At gate → Gate closes) that you can edit or remove.

Decision 3

The screen gets louder as the risk goes up

Problem: both travelers wanted to know what changed, and their disruptions cost them different things: R1 rest, R2 money and missed commitments.

Decision: the Flight tab has three states. A gate change adds a yellow banner and strikes through the old gate. When boarding is at risk, the banner and countdown turn red and give the numbers that matter (gate closes in 15 min, walk 12 min) plus a way to call the airline.

Decision 4

The route is never hidden behind a control

Problem: our strongest finding. Both travelers said the map was easy, but neither found the map control first, and R2 missed the route to the new gate.

Decision: the Route tab opens straight to the route: walk time, a floor diagram and numbered steps with one instruction at a time. After a gate change, the same banner follows you here. Map and camera sit side by side as one switch. In camera mode, the app reads bilingual signs (Japanese and English) and offers “Read sign again” when it can't read one.

04 · Key flows

Three layovers, start to finish

Planning a lounge stop

Picking a place, getting there and getting back, all against the same deadline.

  1. 1 Access rules come first: opening hours and what you need to get in.

  2. 2 The floor change is spelled out (3F, lift, 4F), with the sign text in Japanese and English.

  3. 3 The way back shows the same leave-by time as the plan: 17:01 for a gate that closes at 17:25.

When time runs short

The same flight as the countdown gets tighter. Each state says what's left and what to do next.

  1. 1 25 minutes left: a nudge to head back.

  2. 2 15 minutes left, 12 to walk: the screen turns red and offers to call the airline.

  3. 3 You'd arrive 4 minutes after the gate closes. The app says so plainly and points to the airline, instead of a countdown that can't be met.

Changing airports in Tokyo

Landing at Haneda, leaving from Narita: the transfer is a step of the journey, with its own cost and timing.

  1. 1 The countdown points at the Narita gate, and a banner flags the airport change.

  2. 2 Journey details puts the airport change between the two flights, with the layover length.

  3. 3 The route shows the bus's travel time, price and departure (95 min, ¥3,600, 13:10), so the cost isn't a surprise.

05 · Edge cases & accessibility

Designing for when the plan doesn't hold

A layover app gets used at the worst moments: the place is closed, the gate moved, there isn't enough time. We designed those states on purpose instead of leaving them as error messages.

Too far for the time you have

A trip to another terminal shows its whole cost before you commit: the shuttle there, then the shuttle back, security again and the walk to the gate, which add up to “leave Terminal 2 by 15:53.” When there isn't enough time left, the return screen says so plainly: not enough time for Terminal 2.

Leaving the airside area is treated as its own question too. The screen tells you to ask staff about exiting before you take the shuttle.

Closed when you'd arrive

A planned stop that turns out to be closed shows when it reopens and where that information came from (an airport notice, dated), plus a way to pick another time. It's the “show where information came from and when” rule from our research, applied to a closure.

You missed the banner

Every change also lands in Alerts with the time it happened, the new deadline and the walk, so “what changed and when” is still there after the banner is gone.

Bringing in your tickets

Flights can come in from a PDF, Apple or Google Wallet, Gmail or Google Calendar, or be typed in by hand. Connecting an account says what it reads before you agree, and you can disconnect it later. Our report called for imported information the traveler reviews and can remove; this is where that starts.

Language, routes and color

The language setting offers English, Japanese and French, and sign text appears in both Japanese and English so it matches what's on the wall. Profile has a step-free routes switch for anyone who can't take stairs or escalators. Warnings don't rely on color alone. Each state also has words and numbers, like “Boarding at risk” or “Closed 00:00–04:00.”

06 · Evaluation

What classmates said after using it

Classmates walked through the whole prototype and wrote a critique in the hamburger format: a strength, something to improve, then another strength. We got 14 written critiques. We haven't changed the design based on them yet; this is what they said and what we'd do next.

What worked

  • 6A clean, calm look. One reviewer said calm matters “when traveling is sort of stressful.”
  • 5The leave-by time and the timeline, for judging whether there's time for a stop
  • 4Camera guidance as an alternative to reading a map
  • 2The many ways to bring in a ticket

What didn't

  • 3Asking for features the prototype already has: a route to the gate, a route back from a planned stop, a shuttle transfer between terminals
  • 3Wanting live crowding: how busy a café is, or crowds slowing you down
  • 2The plan was hard to find and check: “I could not tell if the cafe visit was registered in my plan and when.”
  • 2Back buttons were hard to spot (one also found the + button unclear)
  • 2Wanting to scan a ticket with the camera instead of uploading a PDF

The pattern

The interview finding showed up again

Our interviews found that rating something easy didn't mean finding it. In the critiques, three people asked for routes and transfers the prototype already had, and two had trouble finding or checking their plan. The features were there; they weren't visible enough.

What we'd change next

  • Give the plan its own view: a list of your stops with times, so adding, checking and removing one is obvious.
  • Put the route on the plan: make the route from each stop back to the gate one tap from the stop itself.
  • Make navigation controls readable: a labeled + button and a back button that stands out.
